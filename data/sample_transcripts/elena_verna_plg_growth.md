4### [00:02:10] Section: The Definition and Reality of Product-Led Growth (PLG)

**Lenny**: Welcome back to Lenny's Podcast. Today my guest is Elena Verna. Elena is a legendary growth advisor to Miro, Amplitude, SurveyMonkey, Dropbox, and Netlify. Elena, everyone talks about PLG today, but you argue that most companies completely misunderstand what Product-Led Growth actually is. What is PLG really?

**Elena Verna**: Thanks Lenny. Most founders think PLG simply means putting a self-serve "Sign Up for Free" button on their website. That is not PLG; that is just self-serve distribution.

Product-Led Growth is an organizational go-to-market model where the product itself drives acquisition, retention, and monetization. In a true PLG motion, the user experiences the value proposition *before* they are ever asked to pay. The product is the primary vehicle for customer onboarding, user education, usage expansion, and organic referral. If your product requires a human sales rep to explain what it does before someone can get value, you do not have PLG—you have sales-led software with a freemium gimmick.

### [00:11:45] Section: Product-Led Sales (PLS) and Product Qualified Leads (PQLs)

**Lenny**: How should a company transition from pure self-serve PLG to Product-Led Sales (PLS)? When should sales reps get involved?

**Elena Verna**: The biggest mistake companies make is unleashing sales reps on day-one signups. If a user signs up for a free account at 10 AM, and an SDR calls them at 10:05 AM asking for a 30-minute discovery call, you destroy the self-serve trust.

Sales in a PLG model should only engage with **Product Qualified Leads (PQLs)**. A PQL is a user or account that has already crossed the core activation threshold. For example:
- At Miro, a PQL was an account where at least 3 users had collaborated on a board within 7 days and invited an outside colleague.
- At Dropbox, it was when someone shared a folder with 5 team members on the same corporate email domain.

When you pass a PQL to sales, the sales conversation is not: "Let me show you a slide deck of what our product does." It is: "I see your team is already using our product to run weekly sprint planning. Would you like us to help you unlock enterprise SSO, centralized billing, and unlimited storage for the entire department?" That is an expansion conversation, not an annoying cold pitch.

### [00:23:30] Section: The Growth Org: Growth Product vs Growth Marketing

**Lenny**: When should a company hire its first Head of Growth, and how should they structure the team?

**Elena Verna**: Do not hire a Head of Growth before you have proven product-market fit and a baseline retention curve that flattens. If your cohort retention drops to zero, pouring money into growth is just pouring water into a leaky bucket.

Once you have retention, you need two distinct wings in your growth organization:
1. **Growth Product**: Focuses on in-product mechanics: sign-up friction reduction, time-to-value (TTV), viral collaboration loops, and self-serve checkout optimization.
2. **Growth Marketing**: Focuses on scalable top-of-funnel acquisition: paid performance, SEO loops, content distribution, and partner channels.

A common failure mode is hiring a growth marketing person and asking them to fix in-product activation. If they cannot modify the engineering codebase or in-app user onboarding flows, they cannot fix activation.

### [00:36:10] Section: Freemium vs Free Trial

**Lenny**: What is your rule of thumb for choosing between a Freemium tier and a 14-day Free Trial?

**Elena Verna**: Use **Freemium** if your product has viral network effects (like Miro, Figma, or Slack) where free users invite other users, creating a self-reinforcing acquisition loop. Use a **Time-based Free Trial** (like 14 days) if your product has high ongoing infrastructure/compute costs per user, or if the product value is immediately realized by a single user who will never create network effects.
