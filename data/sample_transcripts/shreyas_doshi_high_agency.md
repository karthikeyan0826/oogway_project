### [00:01:15] Section: Introduction and High Agency

**Lenny**: Welcome back to Lenny's Podcast. Today my guest is Shreyas Doshi. Shreyas has led product teams at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo. Shreyas, you often talk about "High Agency" as one of the single most predictive traits of top-tier product builders and executives. How do you define High Agency?

**Shreyas Doshi**: Thanks Lenny. High Agency is fundamentally the refusal to accept that the world is fixed and immutable. A high-agency person looks at a situation where the default answer is "No" or "That is impossible" or "We do not have the resources," and instead of accepting defeat, they ask: "How do we bend reality to make the right outcome happen anyway?"

Most people are low to medium agency. When they encounter an obstacle—say, an engineering team that says a feature will take six months, or legal saying we cannot launch this flow—low-agency people accept that constraint as a law of physics. They say, "Well, legal said no, so we are blocked." A high-agency product manager says: "Let me understand what legal is actually trying to protect against. Let me rewrite the copy or adjust the data retention architecture so the underlying risk disappears, and let us get this launched next Tuesday." High agency people don't ask for permission to solve problems; they find a compliant, creative way forward.

### [00:08:40] Section: The LNO Framework for Time and Energy Management

**Lenny**: That is such a powerful distinction. Another framework that has completely changed how people run their weeks is your LNO framework. Can you break down what LNO stands for?

**Shreyas Doshi**: Yes. LNO stands for Leverage, Neutral, and Overhead. Most PMs are overwhelmed because they treat every single task on their calendar as if it requires 100% perfection. That is a recipe for burnout and mediocre results.

- **L is for Leverage**: These are tasks where an exceptional 10x effort creates a 100x return for the company. Examples include setting your product strategy, designing your core pricing model, or hiring a key design lead. You should dedicate your best energy to L tasks and do them with perfection.
- **N is for Neutral**: These are tasks where the return is linear (1x effort = 1x return). Doing them well is necessary, but doing them with obsessive perfection is a waste of time. Examples include writing weekly status reports, standard sprint planning, or routine feature specs. Do these at a "Good Enough" 80% standard.
- **O is for Overhead**: These are administrative chores that just need to get done so you don't get fired or block others. Examples include filing expense reports, filling out calendar invites, or clearing simple approval tickets. Do these at a 50% standard, as fast as possible, or delegate and automate them.

If you spend 30 hours a week on Overhead and Neutral tasks trying to get an A+ on everything, you will have zero cognitive bandwidth left for your high Leverage tasks.

### [00:19:30] Section: What Separates Good PMs from Great PMs

**Lenny**: In your experience at Stripe and Twitter, what separates a good product manager from a truly great product manager?

**Shreyas Doshi**: A good PM is great at execution: they write clear specs, keep the engineering train on track, manage sprints, and communicate status. 

A great PM has three additional superpowers:
1. **Product Sense and Empathy**: They don't just look at dashboard analytics; they have an intuitive grasp of human psychology, user friction, and unarticulated customer desires.
2. **Strategic Clarity**: They can articulate not just *what* we are building, but *why* this sequence of features creates an unfair, compounding competitive moat over a 3-year horizon.
3. **High-Stakes Communication**: They can walk into a room with skeptical founders or executives and persuade them using crisp, evidence-backed narrative structure rather than 40-slide decks filled with jargon.

### [00:32:15] Section: Tactical Advice on Saying No

**Lenny**: How should a PM say "No" to an executive or CEO without getting sidelined?

**Shreyas Doshi**: Never say a naked "No." Say: "Yes, we can do that, and here is exactly what we will stop doing from our top priority list to make space for it." Frame trade-offs as resource allocation decisions rather than personal resistance. When leadership sees that saying yes to their pet idea pushes back the core revenue project by 3 months, they usually decide against it themselves.
